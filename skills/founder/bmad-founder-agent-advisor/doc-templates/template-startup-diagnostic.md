# {title}

## Table of Contents

## Stage
<!-- One of: no product yet, has users, has paying customers, with the counts and the date. The stage decides which later sections apply and what counts as evidence in each: say which apply and why. When the record and the founder disagree, ask which is right. Done when the founder agrees the stage is what is true today, not what is planned. -->

## Demand
<!-- The strongest evidence that someone would be upset if this disappeared tomorrow. With no product, that is someone already spending time or money on the problem, or committing to pay; with users, what they did when it broke or what they would do without it; with paying customers, renewals, expansion and why anyone left. You are the investor who has heard "everyone loves it" a hundred times: ask for the one person, what they did and when. Push back on waitlists, likes and survey answers by asking what that person gave up to get it. Done when the answer names a person and something they did or paid, or the founder says they have none yet. -->

## Status quo
<!-- What the people with this problem do about it today, and what that costs them in money, hours or risk. "Nothing" often means the problem does not hurt enough; ask what they did the last time it happened. The spreadsheet, the hire or the pile of tools they use now is the real competitor. Done when the workaround and its cost are written down, the cost as a figure or a named open question. -->

## Who needs it most
<!-- The one kind of person who needs this most, specific enough to find: role, type of company or household, the situation that makes it urgent, and a name when the founder has one. "Small businesses" or "busy professionals" is a market, not a person; ask who would pay first or who complained loudest when it broke. Done when someone else could pick this person out of a room. -->

## Smallest paid version
<!-- include when: no paying customers yet. The smallest thing someone would pay for this week, even if it is done by hand behind the scenes. Founders often want to build the whole product first; ask what could be sold before it exists, to whom, and for how much. Done when the version, its price and the first person to offer it to are named. -->

## What surprised you
<!-- include when: the product has users. Something the founder saw while watching a user that they did not expect. No surprise usually means they have not watched closely; ask when they last sat with a user and what that user did first. Done when the answer is something seen, not something said in a survey. -->

## Three years out
<!-- include when: the product has users or paying customers. Whether this becomes more essential or less in three years, and the change in the world that drives it. Ask what would make it unnecessary: a large platform adding it as a feature, a change in the rules, a cheaper way to do the job. Done when the driver is named and the founder has said what would end it. -->

## What the answers show
<!-- Your read, in plain words: which answers rest on evidence, which on hope, and the one question that matters most now. Name the weak point even when the founder is excited; the founder can disagree, and the disagreement is written in. Done when the founder can say the one thing their answers do not yet show. -->

## Assignment
<!-- One task that tests the weakest answer, the date it is due, and the result that would change the plan. Actions in the world only: talk to these five people, ask for payment, watch someone use it, sell the smallest version. "Do more research" and "build the MVP" are not assignments. When the task is a test with a pass mark, name `validation-experiment`; when it is interviews, name `customer-interview-script`. Done when the task, the date and the result that would change the plan are all written. -->
