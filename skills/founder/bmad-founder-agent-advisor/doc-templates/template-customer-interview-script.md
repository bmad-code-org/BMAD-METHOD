<!-- purpose: Give the founder a guide for conversations that show what customers do and pay today, so the answers can be trusted even when the customer is being polite. -->

<!-- stance: A founder who has run hundreds of these and knows people lie kindly about the future. Start from the riskiest assumption these interviews must test, taken from a diagnostic's assignment or a canvas's riskiest assumption when one exists; a question that cannot move it gets cut. Every question asks about the customer's life and about something that already happened; none asks what they would do, would pay or think of the idea. Cut any question that pitches, leads or invites a compliment, and say why. -->

# {title}

## Table of Contents

## What to learn
<!-- The one to three things the founder most needs to learn, each tied to the decision it changes. Ask what answer would make them change course; a goal of "get feedback" learns nothing. Done when each item could come back clearly yes or no. -->

## Who to talk to
<!-- The kind of person, where to find them, how many conversations, and the one or two screening questions that keep the wrong people out. Friends, family and people who cannot buy are out unless they have the problem. Name the first five people or the place they will come from. -->

## What counts as a signal
<!-- Set before the first interview: what a strong signal looks like (they already pay for a workaround, they introduce a colleague, they offer money), what a weak one looks like (compliments, "sounds great", "I would use that"), and how many strong signals out of how many interviews would count as demand. Founders drift into counting enthusiasm; hold them to time, reputation and money. Done when a cofounder reading the notes would score each interview the same way. -->

## Opening
<!-- How the founder asks for the conversation and opens it: they are learning about the customer's work, not selling. The idea stays out until the end, if it comes up at all. -->

## Questions
<!-- The questions in order, about the last time the problem happened: what they did, what it cost, what they have tried, what they pay now, who else was involved. Test each draft question against this: does it ask about the future, a hypothetical or the idea? If so, rewrite it as "Tell me about the last time..." or cut it. Done when no question can be answered with an opinion. -->

## Follow-ups
<!-- The prompts that dig under a first answer: "What happened next?", "Why was that hard?", "What did you do about it?", "Can you show me?", "What did that cost?". When the customer talks about the future or gives a compliment, the follow-up brings them back to a real past case. -->

## Closing ask
<!-- What the founder asks for at the end, sized to the stage: a second conversation, an introduction to someone else with the problem, a look at how they do it now, or with something to sell, a preorder or pilot. A conversation that ends with nothing asked for gives no signal. -->
