## Table of Contents

## Price
<!-- The price in a line or a small table: what the customer buys, what they pay, how often. Write it last. -->

## Buyer and value
<!-- Who pays (the buyer, who may not be the user), the result they get, and what that result is worth to them in money or time. Then what they use today, a competitor, a spreadsheet, a person or nothing, and what it costs them. You are the buyer's finance lead: ask what this replaces in the budget and where the money comes from. When the value comes back as "saves time", ask whose time, how many hours and what an hour costs them. Done when the value and the cost of today's alternative are each a number or a named open question. -->

## Value metric
<!-- The unit the price grows with: per seat, per use, per location, per outcome, or flat. Say why it rises as the customer gets more value, and whether the buyer can predict their bill. Push back on a metric chosen because a competitor uses it. Done when a customer who gets twice the value pays more, and can work out their bill before it arrives. -->

## Evidence customers will pay
<!-- What has been paid, signed, preordered or refused, by whom and at what price. Rank it: money paid beats a signed order, which beats a price discussed on a sales call, which beats interest. You are the investor who has heard a hundred founders say "customers love it": ask for the name and the amount behind each claim, and drop or tag the ones without. With no customers yet, the evidence is what buyers spend today and the first price that will be offered; say so and point to Testing and changing the price. Done when every line names who and what they did. -->

## First customers
<!-- What the first customers pay or will be offered, any discount or pilot terms, and the date those end. Ask what a customer on a free or discounted pilot gives in return and when it turns into the full price. Done when every early deal has a price, an end date for any discount, and the full price it moves to. -->

## Packages
<!-- include when: more than one offer, such as tiers for different customer groups. Each package, who it is for, what it includes, its price, and the one most customers should choose. Ask what makes a customer move up; when nothing does, offer one price. An early company with one customer group usually needs one price. -->

## Unit economics
<!-- include when: serving a customer has a real cost per customer, such as hardware, services, cloud costs or a sales team. Cost to serve one customer, cost to win one, the gross margin at this price, and how long until a customer pays back what it cost to win. Ask how each figure was built; never estimate one. -->

## Testing and changing the price
<!-- How the price will be tested next: a higher price for new customers, a price asked on sales calls, two prices on the page. The signal that it is too low (nobody pushes back, deals close at once) and too high (deals stall on price, not on need), the date it is reviewed, and how existing customers are treated when it changes. Ask when the price last went up; if never, ask what would have to be true to raise it for the next customer. Done when the next test has a date and the result that would move the price either way. -->
