## Table of Contents

## Competitive alternatives
<!-- What the best-fit customer would do if the product did not exist: a competitor, a spreadsheet, hiring someone, doing it by hand, or nothing. Ask what customers used before and what they compared against when buying; when the user names only startups in the same space, ask what most customers do today. Done when the list matches what real customers would say. -->

## Unique value
<!-- What the product has or does that the alternatives do not, what each lets the customer achieve, and the proof: a number, a customer quote, a result. When a claim comes back that any alternative could make ("easy to use", "saves time"), ask what is true only of this product. Done when each claim is unique, stated as what the customer gets, and has proof or is tagged. -->

## Best-fit customer
<!-- The customers who care most about that value, described by traits that make them findable: role, type of company, situation, the event that creates the need. Ask which current customers bought fastest or would be most upset to lose the product, and what they share. -->

## Market category
<!-- The category that tells buyers what to compare the product with and what to expect. Test the obvious category first; propose a new one only when every existing one sets the wrong expectations, and say what teaching it will cost. -->

## Positioning statement
<!-- For [best-fit customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [value]. Unlike [main alternative], it [unique value]. Built from the sections above; it is for the team, not for customers. -->

## One-liner
<!-- The one sentence used on the website, in introductions and in the pitch: what it is and who it is for, in plain words. Offer three versions with a recommendation, and test each by asking whether a best-fit customer would know at once that it is for them. Done when the user would use it unchanged in all three places. -->
