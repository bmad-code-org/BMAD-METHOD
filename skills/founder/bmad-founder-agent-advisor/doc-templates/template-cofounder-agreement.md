# {title}

## Table of Contents

## Goals
<!-- What each co-founder wants from the company: how big, how fast, how long they plan to stay, an outcome they would be happy with, and what they will not do. You are the advisor who has watched co-founders split over goals they never said aloud. Ask each one in turn and compare; when the answers differ, say so plainly and ask whether they can both live with it. Done when each person's answer is in their own words and the differences are named. -->

## Roles
<!-- Who owns what: product, engineering, sales, fundraising, hiring, finance; and the titles. Ask who does each today and who will in a year. When two people claim the same area, or no one claims one, stay on it until it is settled. Done when every area has one owner. -->

## Decisions
<!-- Which decisions each owner makes alone, which need every co-founder, and which will need a board or investors. Name the big ones: raising money, hiring and firing, spending above an amount, changing the product's direction, selling the company. Done when the co-founders could say who decides each of those. -->

## Deadlock
<!-- How a tie breaks on a joint decision: a named tie-breaker for each area, an advisor both trust, a cooling-off period, or a vote. You are the co-founder on the losing side of a split decision: ask each how they would want it handled then, and decide it now. Done when a split between the co-founders has a written way out. -->

## Time and money
<!-- The hours each works now and will work, other jobs and when each goes full time; salaries now and when they start or change; cash each has put in or will, and whether it is a loan, an investment or a gift. Press for dates and amounts; "when we raise" needs an amount or a date. Done when each person's commitment is a number and a date. -->

## Equity split
<!-- The percentage each co-founder holds, and why: what each has brought (work done, code, customers, cash, the idea) and what each will do from here. Weight the work ahead over the work done, since most of the company is still to be built. Push back on an equal split chosen to avoid the conversation, and on an unequal one without stated reasons; offer no numbers of your own. Ask whether each would still call the split fair if another worked half as hard as planned. Done when each co-founder can give the reason for their own share and accepts the others'. -->

## Vesting
<!-- How each co-founder earns their shares over time: the length, any cliff before the first shares vest, credit for time already worked, and what speeds vesting up, such as a sale of the company. Four years with a one-year cliff is common at venture-backed US startups; ask the lawyer what fits where they are. Done when each co-founder knows what they would keep if they left at month six, year two and year four. -->

## If someone leaves
<!-- What happens when a co-founder leaves by choice, is asked to leave, stops performing, or cannot work: to their vested and unvested shares, their role, the work and code they made, and their pay. You are the co-founder who leaves in year two, then the one who stays: ask each side what they would call fair. Ask how "stops performing" would be decided, since that is the case co-founders avoid. Done when each kind of leaving has a stated outcome. -->

## Revisiting
<!-- optional. When the co-founders will review this agreement: a date, or an event such as a funding round or a new co-founder joining. -->

## Questions for a lawyer
<!-- Every legal, tax and securities point as a question, such as how the shares are issued and vested, who owns work done before the company existed, any tax filing that follows a share grant (a US 83(b) election, for example), and how the leaving terms are written. List the points not yet agreed; the lawyer needs them settled before drafting. Done when a lawyer could start drafting from this document and these questions. -->
