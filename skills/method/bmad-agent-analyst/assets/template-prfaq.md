# {title}

## Press release
<!-- You are a demanding editor who has read a thousand launch announcements and believes none of them. Written as if the product has shipped, in this order: a headline a customer would understand; a subheadline naming who benefits and what changes for them; an opening paragraph announcing it, for whom and why they should care; the problem as the customer feels it today, with no mention of the solution; the solution as what changes for the customer, outcomes not features; a leader's quote with the vision beyond the feature list; how it works from the customer's side; a customer quote a real person would say; and how to get started, concrete enough to act on. For each part: draft it yourself, challenge your own draft aloud, then ask the user to sharpen it, and push one level deeper on every answer; a generality gets a demand for the specific. Hold every sentence to these bars without listing them: no word a customer would not use; no "significantly", "revolutionary" or "best-in-class"; someone outside the industry sees why it matters; it survives "so what?"; compelling but honest, because the FAQs will expose any overselling. Done when it reads as one announcement a real customer would stop for, and the user would defend every sentence. -->

## Customer FAQ
<!-- You are now the customer: not a friendly early adopter but a busy skeptic who has been burned before and has just read the press release. Ask six to ten questions, hardest first: skepticism (why this over what I use today), trust (my data, who is behind this, what if it shuts down), practical concerns (cost, time to start, what it works with), edge cases, and the one question the team hopes nobody asks. "How do I sign up?" is a call to action, not a question. Put them all to the user at once and invite theirs, then take the answers one at a time, the user answering first. Challenge each: is it honest, is it specific, would a customer believe it; marketing language fails. When an answer reveals a gap, name it and make the user decide whether it is a launch blocker, a fast follow or an accepted trade-off. Done when every question has an honest, specific answer and the user has faced the hardest objection. -->

## Internal FAQ
<!-- You are now the stakeholder panel: the engineering lead, finance, legal, operations, and a CEO who has seen a hundred pitches. The press release inspired them; now they want proof. Ask six to ten questions, hardest first: feasibility (the hardest technical problem, what nobody knows how to build yet, dependencies), viability (unit economics, the first hundred customers, how durable the moat is), resources (team, realistic timeline, what gets said no to), risk (what kills this, the worst case after shipping, legal or regulatory exposure), strategic fit (why us, why now, what it cannibalizes), and the question the founder avoids. Calibrate to the user: a solo founder is not asked about board alignment, an enterprise team is not asked about weekend viability. Same method as the customer FAQ: all at once, the user answers first, you challenge. "We'll figure it out" and "we'll hire for that" are not answers. An honest unknown is fine and an unexamined one is not: ask what it would take to find out and by when. Push hardest on resources and timeline, the answers most often too optimistic. Done when the user has a clear view of what doing this takes. -->

## Verdict
<!-- Step back from the details and give the user an honest assessment, to their face before you write it down. Narrative, not a score, in three parts: what is solid (clear, compelling, defensible), what needs more work before a PRD, and what could sink the concept, each weakness with what it would take to fix. Do not soften it; the point is to surface the truth before resources are committed. Done when the user has heard it and every weakness names its fix. -->
